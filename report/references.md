## 2. Evidence for each reference

### 2.1 Apple (n.d.), *Accessibility*, Human Interface Guidelines
- **Link:** https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility
- **Checked:** read on the Apple Developer site, 8 Nov 2026 (the page's latest change note is dated June 2025). Web pages without a date take "n.d." plus a retrieval date in APA 7.
- **Finding:** For iOS the page gives 44 × 44 pt as the default control size and 28 × 28 pt as the minimum, and asks for padding between controls (about 12 pt around bezelled elements, 24 pt otherwise). It asks apps to let people enlarge text, "ideally ... by at least 200 percent", either through the app's own controls or Dynamic Type, to reduce automatic animation when Reduce Motion is on, and to give text up to 17 pt a contrast of at least 4.5:1.
- **Used for:** F2 44 pt ☆ and row targets; F5 text size 100–150–200% as an in-app control, which supports our position on test finding 10 (text size under More; 7 of 9 participants went to More first); F5 calm mode (Reduce Motion); the contrast audit in §3.7.

### 2.2 Brakewood & Watkins (2019)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2018.1472147
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record; abstract and issue details (vol. 39, no. 3, May 2019) on RePEc/IDEAS, 8 Nov 2026. The DOI was registered on online publication in 2018, but APA 7 uses the issue year, 2019.
- **Finding:** A review of studies on what real-time information (RTI) gives passengers. The main behavioural effects are shorter waits, shorter journeys through better route choice and more use of transit; RTI may also raise satisfaction and the feeling of personal security. The authors name actual route-choice change and post-implementation cost–benefit analysis as open gaps.
- **Used for:** F1 arrival-first Home; F4 next bus on every nearby stop; widens theme A beyond the single Seattle project.

### 2.3 Chandler & Sweller (1991)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0804_2
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record; publisher abstract (via OpenAlex), 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** Six experiments with technical training material. Besides the cost of splitting related information across sources, three experiments found that adding explanatory material that seemed useful but was not essential made learning worse, even when it was well integrated (the redundancy effect).
- **Used for:** discussion of test finding 6 (walking time understood by 3/9): the recommended F3A card shows the walk and journey times several times over (headline total, "Walk 2 min" chip, and the step list open by default). Supports collapsing the steps by default as future work. Caveat: the evidence comes from learning tasks, which we extend to glanceable reading.

### 2.4 Chen & Chan (2014)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2014.895855
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record; publisher abstract (via OpenAlex), 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** A questionnaire survey of 1,012 Hong Kong residents aged 55 and over tested a senior technology acceptance model (STAM), which explained 68% of the variance in technology use. Age, gender, education, technology self-efficacy and anxiety, health and ability, and facilitating conditions predicted use directly, and did so better than the usual measures of perceived usefulness and ease of use.
- **Used for:** the older-passenger target group (§1, §3.2); F5 text size and contrast (ability) and calm mode (anxiety); the limitation that our test was run in English with what was probably a sample of fellow students. It is the only Hong Kong study in the list: we found no peer-reviewed usability study of a Hong Kong bus app.

### 2.5 Czaja et al. (2019)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1201/b22189
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record (CRC Press, 2019); title, subtitle, edition and table of contents on the publisher's Routledge/CRC page, 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** A standard human-factors text that gives design guidelines for older adults. Its chapters cover the characteristics of older adults and individual differences, basic design principles, interface design and the user experience, transportation, and involving older adults in design research.
- **Used for:** framing older passengers as a design group (§1, §3.2); F5; the limitation and future work on testing with participants aged 60+. **Caution:** we have not checked specific numbers in the book (for example a recommended target size). Check the page in the library copy before attaching any figure to this citation.

### 2.6 Edwards, Li & Lee (2002)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2002.10673678
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record (pp. 83–95); article first page (Journal of Advertising, Vol. 31, No. 3, Fall 2002) and results section read in a scanned copy, 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** In an experiment with forced pop-up adverts, ads felt more intrusive to people who were deeply engaged in what they were doing, and less intrusive when the ad related to their task; ad length (10 or 20 s) made no difference. Perceived intrusiveness led to irritation and to avoiding the ad.
- **Used for:** F1: no launch or pop-up adverts, one labelled and closable ad card; F5 calm mode removes it; the launch-advert pain point (−3) in the journey map.

### 2.7 Ferris, Watkins & Borning (2010)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753597
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record (CHI '10, pp. 1807–1816); full text read in the authors' copy (University of Washington), 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** A survey of 488 users of OneBusAway, Seattle's real-time bus information service. 92% were more satisfied with public transit, 91% spent less time waiting and 78% were more likely to walk to a different stop. The biggest complaint (25%) was inaccurate data, and users asked for better handling of the stops they use often. Satisfaction gains were larger for younger respondents, and most respondents were aged 25–34.
- **Used for:** F1 saved routes and next arrivals first; F1 Live/Scheduled labels (trust depends on knowing which times are real-time); F2 saving frequently used stops; F4 walking time and next bus on each stop.

### 2.8 Findlater et al. (2013)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2470703
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record (CHI '13, pp. 343–346); full text read in the authors' copy (University of Washington), 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** 20 older (61–86) and 20 younger (19–51) adults did pointing, dragging, crossing and steering tasks with a mouse and on an iPad. Older adults were slower overall, but the touchscreen cut their movement time by 35% compared with the mouse (16% for younger adults), and error rates fell. Dragging was relatively slow on the touchscreen.
- **Used for:** F2 save and Undo as single taps, with no drag or swipe needed; larger targets for older users. Caveat: the iPad lay flat on a table, unlike a phone used one-handed at a bus stop.

### 2.9 Fitts (1954)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1037/h0055392
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record (APA), 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** In tapping and transfer tasks, the time to hit a target grows with its distance and shrinks with its width; Fitts expressed this as an index of difficulty, log2(2A/W).
- **Used for:** F2 ☆ on every row and a full-width labelled Save button; primary actions as large buttons; the bottom tab bar; the "before" analysis of App1933's save action hidden behind small icons.

### 2.10 Gray et al. (2018)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174108
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record (CHI '18, pp. 1–14) and paper number 534; abstract; strategy definitions on the authors' lab site (UXP2, Purdue University), 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** A content analysis of 118 practitioner-identified dark patterns, grouped into five strategies: nagging, obstruction, sneaking, interface interference and forced action. Nagging is a redirection of the user's expected task that persists across interactions, often as pop-ups that cover the interface.
- **Used for:** F1 removal of the launch advert (nagging); the "before" analysis of App1933's interstitial and banner adverts.

### 2.11 Hick (1952)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1080/17470215208416600
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record; publisher abstract (via OpenAlex), 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** In choice-reaction experiments with up to ten alternatives, reaction time rose with the amount of information in the choice, which grows with the logarithm of the number of alternatives; people gained information at roughly five bits per second.
- **Used for:** F5 three headed groups instead of about 30 unsorted icons; five labelled tabs; our position against adding a sixth map tab (test finding 8). Caveat: the experiments used equally likely, unpractised choices.

### 2.12 Nielsen (1994)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1145/191666.191729
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record (CHI '94, pp. 152–158); origin of the revised heuristics confirmed on the Nielsen Norman Group heuristics page, 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** Nielsen factor-analysed 249 usability problems to find the heuristics with the most explanatory power; this is the empirical basis of the ten heuristics (H1–H10) used across the report.
- **Used for:** H1 Live/Scheduled labels and save feedback; H2 the "Full route" label (finding 2); H3 Undo; H4 text size in settings (finding 10); H6 labelled tabs and ☆; H7 Home first; H8 no clutter and the walking-time card (finding 6).

### 2.13 Norman (2013)
- **ISBN:** 978-0-465-05065-9 (no DOI)
- **Checked:** publisher page (Basic Books, "Revised and Expanded Edition", 5 Nov 2013, 368 pp.); table of contents on the author's site (jnd.org), which states that signifiers are the main conceptual addition to this edition, 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** Chapter 1 sets out the fundamental principles of interaction: affordances, signifiers, mapping, feedback and conceptual models. The revised edition separates affordances (what actions are possible) from signifiers (perceivable cues that show where and how to act).
- **Used for:** F2 labelled 收藏此站 button and ☆ on every stop; test finding 3 (the Home route card was tappable but showed no signifier), our main accepted weakness and the basis of the future direction.

### 2.14 Parhi, Karlson & Bederson (2006)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1145/1152215.1152260
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record (MobileHCI '06, pp. 203–210); abstract on the Microsoft Research publication page, 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** A two-phase study of one-handed thumb tapping. Error rates did not differ significantly for targets of 9.6 mm or more in single taps and 7.7 mm or more in tap sequences; the authors recommend 9.2 mm for single (discrete) targets and 9.6 mm for serial tasks.
- **Used for:** F2 ☆ and row targets; F4 stop cards; commuters' one-handed use. By our calculation, 44 pt is about 7.3 mm on a current iPhone Pro (3 px per pt at about 460 ppi), so primary actions should be larger than 44 pt where space allows.

### 2.15 Shneiderman et al. (2016)
- **ISBN:** 978-0-13-438038-4 (hardcover; no DOI)
- **Checked:** Pearson catalogue (6th edition, six authors, 2016); the eight golden rules on Shneiderman's University of Maryland page, which cites section 3.3.4 of this edition, 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** The eight golden rules include offering informative feedback, preventing errors, keeping users in control and, as rule 6, permitting easy reversal of actions, because reversibility "relieves anxiety".
- **Used for:** F2 Undo instead of a confirmation dialog; F3 the visible, reversible typo correction (F3C); item-by-item removal with Undo in Saved.

### 2.16 Watkins et al. (2011)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tra.2011.06.010
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record; abstract on RePEc/IDEAS, 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** At Seattle bus stops, riders without real-time information thought they had waited longer than they had, while riders using it did not. Real-time information cut perceived waits by about 0.7 min (13%), and its users actually waited almost two minutes less.
- **Used for:** F1 next arrival as the largest item on each saved route; Live labels; test finding 1 (9/9 read the next Bus 8 correctly).

### 2.17 World Wide Web Consortium (2024), WCAG 2.2
- **Link:** https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ (this version: https://www.w3.org/TR/2024/REC-WCAG22-20241212/)
- **Checked:** w3.org, 8 Nov 2026. The version at the stable link is the W3C Recommendation of 12 December 2024, an errata update of the first Recommendation of 5 October 2023 (W3C news, 5 Oct 2023).
- **Finding:** Success criteria we rely on: 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), 4.5:1 (AA); 1.4.4 Resize Text, up to 200% without loss of content (AA); 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum), 24 × 24 CSS px (AA); 2.5.5 Target Size (Enhanced), 44 × 44 CSS px (AAA); 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions, motion can be disabled (AAA); 3.3.4 Error Prevention, which accepts a reversible submission as one of three safeguards (AA). The drafts also cite 1.1.1 Non-text Content, 1.3.1 Info and Relationships, 1.4.1 Use of Color and 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide, all part of WCAG 2.2.
- **Used for:** F5 text size to 200%; the contrast and target-size audit (§3.7); F5 calm mode; F2 Undo as the reversible option; F1 Live/Scheduled shown by text as well as colour. Caveat: WCAG is written for web content; we apply it to an app by analogy (CSS px ≈ pt).

### 2.18 Zaphiris, Ghiawadwala & Mughal (2005)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1145/1056808.1057050
- **Checked:** Crossref DOI record (CHI '05 Extended Abstracts, pp. 1897–1900); full text read in a copy hosted by Dublin City University, 8 Nov 2026.
- **Finding:** From a review of over 100 papers on ageing and HCI, the authors drew 52 guidelines, then used card sorting (40 students) and an expert focus group to reduce them to 38 guidelines in 11 categories, checked by heuristic evaluation of two websites. They include larger targets with clear confirmation; no animation or moving text; no pop-up or animated adverts; 12–14 pt sans-serif text; high foreground–background contrast; a shallow hierarchy grouped into meaningful categories; fewer choices; no irrelevant information; search that copes with spelling errors; and support for user control and freedom.
- **Used for:** F1 no pop-ups; F2 larger targets and visible confirmation (filled ☆, button label change, toast); F3 spelling correction (F3C); F5 grouped menu, text size, high contrast, calm mode; finding 6 (irrelevant repetition on the result card). Caveat: guidelines for desktop websites in 2005, validated by heuristic evaluation rather than with older users.

## 3. Related Work: themes and synthesis

Suggested order for Section 2. Each theme ends with the design consequence.

### A. Real-time information and trust (F1, F4)
*Ferris et al. (2010); Watkins et al. (2011); Brakewood & Watkins (2019)*

The sources agree that real-time arrival information shortens waits, both measured and perceived, and raises satisfaction, and Brakewood and Watkins's review shows the same pattern across the wider literature, not only in Seattle. The evidence is narrower than it looks, though: two of the three studies come from the same OneBusAway project with mostly young, self-selected users, Ferris et al. found smaller satisfaction gains among older respondents, and inaccurate data was users' biggest complaint. The benefit therefore depends on riders trusting the numbers, and it does not reach older riders by default. We found no peer-reviewed usability study of a Hong Kong bus app, so we treat these results as principles to transfer, not local proof.
→ so in F1 we put saved routes and their next arrival first and label every time 實時 Live or 預定 Scheduled so riders know which numbers to trust, and in F4 we show walking time and the next buses on each nearby stop so riders can decide whether to walk to another stop. In the test, 9/9 read the next Bus 8 correctly.

### B. Interruptions, adverts and dark patterns (F1, F5)
*Edwards et al. (2002); Gray et al. (2018); Zaphiris et al. (2005)*

Edwards et al. show that pop-ups feel most intrusive when people are focused on a task, which is exactly the moment a rider opens a bus app, and that intrusiveness leads to irritation and avoidance. Gray et al. treat such persistent interruptions ("nagging") as an ethical problem, not only a usability one, and the ageing guidelines of Zaphiris et al. independently rule out pop-up and animated adverts. The sources differ on the remedy: Edwards et al. found task-related ads less intrusive, so the evidence argues against forced interruption rather than against advertising as such, which matters because the operator relies on advertising income.
→ so in F1 we removed the launch advert and all pop-ups but kept one labelled, closable "Ad · Sponsored" card at the end of Home, and in F5 calm mode removes it entirely.

### C. Choice, density and redundancy (F3, F5, tab bar)
*Hick (1952); Nielsen (1994); Chandler & Sweller (1991); Zaphiris et al. (2005)*

Hick's law, Nielsen's minimalist-design heuristic (H8) and the ageing guidelines of Zaphiris et al. ("provide fewer choices", "avoid irrelevant information") reach the same advice from experiment, practice and literature review. Hick's experiments used equally likely, unpractised choices, so in a familiar menu grouping and clear labels matter as much as the raw number of items, which is why we grouped settings rather than only cutting them. Chandler and Sweller add a sharper warning: even information that seems useful harms understanding when it is not essential. Our lowest comprehension result fits this: only 3/9 picked out the 2-minute walk on a card that repeats its time values, though the question's wording may also have played a part.
→ so in F5 we replaced about 30 unsorted icons with three headed groups and kept five tabs, declining a sixth map tab; in F3 the recommended card leads with the total time people decide on, and collapsing its step list by default is a future change.

### D. Signifiers, feedback and reversible actions (F2, F3)
*Norman (2013); Shneiderman et al. (2016); Nielsen (1994); W3C (2024)*

Norman separates what an interface allows (affordances) from what it shows (signifiers): an action can exist and still go unused if nothing signals it. Shneiderman et al.'s rule on easy reversal, Nielsen's user control and freedom (H3) and WCAG 3.3.4, which accepts a reversible action in place of a confirmation, agree that undo is the better safeguard for everyday, low-stakes actions. Undo only helps once the user has found the action, however, and our save task shows the gap: once on the route screen, participants saved without trouble, but only 2/9 took the expected path there, because the tappable route card on Home had no signifier.
→ so in F2 we gave saving two visible signifiers (a labelled 收藏此站 button and a ☆ on every stop) with immediate feedback and Undo instead of a dialog, and in F3 the typo correction says what it changed and can be undone. We report the missing signifier on the Home card as our main design weakness.

### E. Touch targets and ageing motor control (F2, F4, all screens)
*Fitts (1954); Parhi et al. (2006); Findlater et al. (2013); Apple (n.d.); W3C (2024)*

Fitts's law and Parhi et al. agree that bigger targets are faster and safer, and Parhi et al. recommend about 9.2 mm for a single thumb tap. The platform and web standards set lower bars, with Apple's 44 × 44 pt (about 7 mm on a current iPhone, by our calculation) and WCAG's 24 px minimum and 44 px enhanced size, so meeting a guideline is not the same as meeting the empirical recommendation. Findlater et al. add that touch narrows the gap between older and younger adults but does not close it, and that dragging is slow, which favours large single taps over gestures; their tablet set-up, though, is easier than a phone held in one hand at a bus stop.
→ so in F2 we put a 44 pt ☆ on every stop and a full-width labelled Save button as the larger primary target, made save and Undo single taps, and kept navigation in the bottom tab bar; 90% of tappable elements on the core screens are at least 44 pt, and none is below 24 pt.

### F. Older adults: legibility, acceptance and accessibility settings (F5)
*Czaja et al. (2019); Zaphiris et al. (2005); Chen & Chan (2014); W3C (2024); Apple (n.d.)*

The ageing literature and the standards point the same way: larger sans-serif text, strong contrast and little motion (Zaphiris et al.), 4.5:1 contrast and text that resizes to 200% (WCAG), and an option to enlarge text ideally to at least 200%, through the app's own control or the system setting, with less animation when Reduce Motion is on (Apple). Chen and Chan's survey of 1,012 older Hong Kong residents adds that ability, self-efficacy and anxiety predict technology use better than perceived usefulness, so these accommodations and a calm interface are central, not optional extras. Two cautions apply: the Zaphiris et al. guidelines were written for desktop websites in 2005, and Czaja et al. give a whole chapter to involving older adults in design research, which our test, run in English with what was probably a sample of fellow students, has not yet done.
→ so in F5 we put text size (100%, 150%, 200%) at the top of More, with high contrast and calm mode beside it. In the test, 7 of 9 participants went to More first, and after enlarging the text all 9 read the Bus 116 arrival correctly at 200%.
